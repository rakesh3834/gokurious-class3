What’s going on here?

A poster needs words that people can notice and read. A heading tells the reader what the poster is about. Body text gives the smaller details. Formatting changes the appearance of text, such as its size, colour or style. It does not need to change the letters or the message those letters spell.

Our poster begins with OUR GARDEN above two short body lines. Select the heading by choosing it. A selected border shows that the heading is the text you intend to format. If you use a formatting control before selecting it, the model asks you to select first. This helps you connect a change with the correct target.

Use Heading size to make the heading larger or smaller within a readable range. Try the simple font and the book font. A font is a style of letter shapes. The words still say OUR GARDEN, even though the shapes of the letters can look different. Compare the heading with the unchanged body text after each small adjustment.

Bold makes the letters heavier. Italic gives them a slant. Underline adds a line beneath them. Each control can be switched on and off, so you can compare its effect. You can also choose one of three dark colours that remain clear on the light background. A useful poster needs readable contrast as well as a noticeable heading.

Try changing one feature at a time before combining several. A larger, bold heading may be enough for your design; every feature does not need to be switched on. Read the status caption to check which styles are active. Explain which change made the heading easier to notice and show that its words and the body text stayed the same. Reset restores the original appearance so you can compare another design from the same starting point.

Little questions, big discoveries

What is bold?

Bold makes letters look heavier. It can help a heading stand out from smaller body text.

Can I use more than one style?

Yes. Try one at a time, then combine useful choices while keeping the heading easy to read.

Why did the body text not change?

This model formats only the selected heading. The body provides an unchanged comparison.

Talk about it: parents & teachers

At home

  • Compare two heading designs and ask your child to explain a readability choice.
  • Let your child explain one change in the model before offering help. Give time for another attempt.

In the classroom

  • Have learners change one feature at a time and name the visible effect before combining styles.
  • Ask pairs to predict, take turns with the controls, and describe the evidence they observed. Use the three challenges to identify a point to revisit.
