What’s going on here?

Some materials let us see a scene clearly through them. Others allow only a blurred view, and some hide the scene completely. Window Wonder compares three selected samples in front of the same picture. Keeping the picture unchanged helps us focus on what the sample does to our view.

Choose clear glass and slide it in front. You can still make out the tree and house clearly. A material we can see through clearly is called transparent. Now choose the thin translucent paper. Light and blurred shapes are visible, but the small details are not clear. This partly see-through result is called translucent. The word describes the view through this particular sample.

Choose wood next. The wooden panel hides the picture behind it. A material we cannot see through is called opaque. The tree and house have not disappeared; they are covered from our view. Move the panel away to check that the same picture is still there. Then slide it back and compare again before choosing the matching label.

A material’s name alone does not always tell the whole story. Glass can be clear or frosted, and paper can differ in thickness. Our labels apply to the samples described in the controls. The thin paper here lets light through, while a thick card might block the view. In everyday life, people choose different materials for windows, screens and covers according to what they want to see. You can compare safe materials against an ordinary picture with an adult. Never look at the Sun or a bright lamp for this activity. No flame, loose glass or special equipment is needed to practise the three visibility words.

Make it real

See-through picture test

You need: An ordinary picture, tracing paper and thick card, with adult help.

  1. Look at the picture directly.
  2. Place tracing paper in front and describe the view.
  3. Cover it with card, then uncover it.

Use safe materials and ordinary light. Never look at the Sun or a bright lamp.

Little questions, big discoveries

Is all glass transparent?

No. Clear glass is our transparent sample; other glass can give a blurred view.

Is all paper translucent?

No. Thickness and the kind of paper affect what you can see through it.

Does opaque mean the hidden object is gone?

No. It means you cannot see the object through that sample.

Talk about it: parents & teachers

At home

  • Ask the child to compare the same picture through each sample.
  • Use card and tracing paper rather than loose glass.

In the classroom

  • Use clear, partly visible and hidden views before introducing the three terms.
  • Avoid ray diagrams and refraction.
