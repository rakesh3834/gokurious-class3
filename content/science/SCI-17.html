What’s going on here?

A stone, water and air behave differently when we move them. A stone keeps its own shape in our ordinary handling example. Water flows and takes the shape of the lower part of a container. Air can flow into and out of open spaces. Shape and Flow Lab uses these three familiar examples to explore solids, liquids and gases.

Choose the stone and move it into the tall cup. Its place changes, but its outline stays the same. Repeat with the wide bowl. The stone still has the same outline. It is our solid example. The lesson does not claim that every solid is impossible to bend or reshape. We are describing what happens to this stone when it is simply moved.

Choose water next. Pour it into the tall cup, then compare the wide bowl. The same amount looks deeper in the narrow container and shallower in the wide one. The water fits the space it occupies instead of keeping the shape of its first position. Water is our liquid example. The drawing is a simplified flat view rather than a measuring scale.

Finally choose air. The blue movement marks help show something we cannot usually see. They are symbols for flow, not pictures of tiny air particles. Air can move through an open container instead of forming a settled water layer at the bottom. Air is a gas even though it is invisible. These observations give us useful comparisons without needing a particle model. When you describe a trial, name the material, explain what you changed and say what happened. A safe paper drawing can record all three comparisons without pouring anything or handling unknown substances.

Learning outcomeI can compare a stone, water and air using simple observations.

Little questions, big discoveries

Can I see the air itself as blue lines?

No. The lines are teaching symbols for movement.

Does water become solid in a cup?

No. It remains a liquid in this ordinary pouring example.

Does solid mean nothing can ever change its shape?

No. This lesson observes a stone being moved, not every possible force or solid.

Talk about it: parents & teachers

At home

  • Ask the child to describe shape separately from location.
  • Use the model when a real pouring activity is inconvenient.

In the classroom

  • Use clear examples: stone, water and air.
  • Avoid a particle model and do not label visible smoke or steam as pure gas.
