What’s going on here?

A tree can be a busy place even when it seems still. Animals may come for food, a place to rest or a sheltered space for their young. The same plant can help different visitors in different ways. In Tree Neighbourhood, you decide which features to place before an animal arrives. Watch for the particular need shown in each visit.

Choose the caterpillar and add leaves. On its visit, the caterpillar feeds on a leaf. Choose the monkey and add fruit instead. The monkey’s visit shows another food relationship. These examples do not mean that every animal eats every plant part. An animal needs food that suits it, so a fruit cannot simply replace the leaf in our caterpillar example.

Flowers can provide nectar, a sweet liquid visited by butterflies. A tree hollow has another use. It can offer an owl shelter and a nesting place. The hollow is not a meal. This difference helps us explain how a plant is useful rather than just listing animals near it. Food, shelter and resting places are different kinds of help.

Remove the hollow and let the owl visit again. The model shows that this tree does not meet the owl’s selected need. It does not claim the owl has no possible home anywhere else. Another suitable place might be nearby. Real trees also take time to grow their features; our controls assemble a learning picture instantly. When you observe a plant with an adult, notice what an animal is actually doing. Watching quietly from a distance is better than disturbing a nest, touching an animal or offering unfamiliar food.

Make it real

Food or shelter picture pairs

You need: Paper and pencils.

  1. Draw a leaf, a fruit, a flower and a tree hollow.
  2. Draw or name one matching animal visitor for each.
  3. Add Food or Shelter beside the pairs.

Explain the animal’s action, not just its name. Observe real animals from a safe distance.

Little questions, big discoveries

Do animals use plants only for food?

No. Plants can also provide shelter, nesting places and places to rest.

Is nectar the same as a leaf?

No. Nectar is a sweet liquid found in many flowers.

Should we open a nest to see inside?

No. Watch quietly from a distance and leave nests undisturbed.

Talk about it: parents & teachers

At home

  • Ask which plant feature met the visitor’s need.
  • Use verbs such as feeding, resting and nesting.

In the classroom

  • Let children explain one direct plant–animal relationship.
  • Avoid expanding the activity into food-web calculations.
