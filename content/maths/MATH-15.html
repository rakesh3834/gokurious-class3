What’s going on here?

A container’s capacity tells us how much it can hold. To compare containers fairly, we can use the same measuring cup or the same amount of water. This activity has a jug that holds one litre. Choose a one-litre, half-litre or quarter-litre cup, then count each full cup poured into the jug.

Two full half-litre cups make one litre. Four full quarter-litre cups also make one litre. One full litre cup fills it in a single pour. The final amount is the same in all three trials, but the number of pours differs because the cup size changes. Smaller cups need more repeats to reach the same total.

The jug view can change from tall and narrow to short and wide. This moves the same water into another one-litre container without adding or spilling any. The wide jug shows a lower level because its water spreads across more space. A higher water level does not always mean more water when the containers have different shapes. Count the known cupfuls to check.

Keep the measuring cup the same size during one trial. If you change the cup choice, the activity starts again with an empty jug so the pour count has a clear meaning. The button stops when another full cup would not fit. Real pouring needs care: use unbreakable containers, a small tray and adult help where needed. Try comparing an equal amount in two different cups before deciding which looks fuller. Say both the number of pours and the cup size when describing your result. Four pours alone is not enough information, since four small cups and four large cups can hold very different amounts. A shared measuring unit makes the comparison useful.

Make it real

Same water, new shape

You need: Two unbreakable containers, one small cup, water and a tray

  1. Pour two equal cupfuls into one container.
  2. Transfer all the water into the other container.
  3. Compare the heights and explain why the amount stayed the same.

Ask an adult to help with spills. Do not drink experiment water; reuse it for a suitable household purpose.

Little questions, big discoveries

Does a tall glass always hold more?

No. Width and shape also matter; compare using a common amount or measure.

Why does a smaller cup need more pours?

Each pour adds less water, so more repeats reach the same litre.

Why does changing cups empty the jug?

It starts a fresh trial with one consistent cup size.

Talk about it: parents & teachers

At home

  • Ask for the cup size as well as the pour count.
  • Compare equal amounts in differently shaped unbreakable cups.

In the classroom

  • Use whole, half and quarter litres as concrete amounts.
  • Avoid millilitre conversions and volume formulas.
