What’s going on here?

A number pattern can be made by repeating the same change. Start at 100 and add ten. You reach 110. Add ten again and reach 120. Continue with the same jump and the next landing will be 130. The starting number and the repeated change together tell the story of the pattern. Read both before making your first prediction.

The track covers future landing numbers with question marks. Enter a prediction and reveal one jump. Compare your answer with the number that appears, then use the rule again. The picture spaces are steps in the story, not a ruler for measuring the numerical distance. A larger jump changes the numbers more even though the circles remain in the same places.

Try adding twenty instead of ten while keeping the start at 100. The first landing becomes 120, followed by 140 and 160. Each jump is now ten larger than before, but the change between neighbouring numbers in this new pattern stays twenty. Do not switch rules partway through a prediction. A fixed jump means repeating exactly the same addition or subtraction.

Subtraction patterns work in the other direction. Start at 200 and subtract twenty each time to reach 180, 160 and 140. Each number is smaller than the one before. Our activity stays between zero and 1000 and shows at most five jumps. If a further jump would leave that range, the reveal button stops. Changing the start or jump begins a new track. Use the observations to explain mistakes: name the previous number, the chosen change and the correct next number. Patterns can be explored with spoken counting, paper cards and steps along a floor line. There is no need for a complicated formula when you can follow and explain the repeated change.

Make it real

Hidden landing cards

You need: Six paper cards and pencil

  1. Write a start number on one card.
  2. Choose a jump of ten or twenty and write five landing cards.
  3. Cover future cards and ask a partner to predict them.

Use a table or clear indoor floor; no actual jumping is required.

Little questions, big discoveries

Must a pattern start at zero?

No. Choose any starting number that lets your jumps stay within the range.

Why does the reveal button stop?

Either five jumps are complete or the next jump would leave 0–1000.

Does every question mark stand for the same number?

No. It hides the next landing reached by the repeated change.

Talk about it: parents & teachers

At home

  • Ask the child to say the rule before revealing.
  • Discuss a mistaken prediction using the previous landing.

In the classroom

  • Compare fixed addition and subtraction patterns.
  • Keep numbers whole and within 0–1000; avoid algebraic sequence rules.
