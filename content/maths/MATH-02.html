What’s going on here?

A toy can be made from several solid shapes put together. A box may look like a cuboid, a ball like a sphere, and a pointed party hat like a cone. In the workshop, choose a shape and join it to the toy. You can replace the last part instead of making the toy longer. Describe which shapes are beside one another.

Look closely at the surfaces. A cube has six flat square faces. A cuboid also has six flat faces, but they do not all have to be squares. A cube is a special kind of cuboid. This means that calling a cube a cuboid is allowed. The more specific name cube tells us extra information about its faces.

A cylinder has two flat circular faces and a curved surface around them. A cone has one flat circular face and a curved surface leading to its point. A sphere has a curved surface all around and no flat face. Switch on the highlight to find flat surfaces in the drawing. A drawing can only show some surfaces at once.

Turning the toy changes the way its parts face, but it does not turn a cylinder into a cube. Replacing a part really changes the list of shapes. Try replacing a cylinder with a cube and explain the difference in surfaces. Real toys may have rounded edges, holes or decorations, so they only resemble these simple shapes. Our pictures leave such details out to help you compare. When you describe a construction to a friend, name the shape and its position. That gives your friend enough clues to build a matching toy.

Learning outcomeI can identify shapes in a toy and describe their faces.

Make it real

Describe my toy

You need: Clean empty boxes and a soft ball

  1. Arrange two boxes and a ball on a table.
  2. Describe each shape and where you placed it.
  3. Ask a partner to copy the arrangement from your description.

Use light, clean materials. Do not stack heavy objects or use sharp cans.

Little questions, big discoveries

Can a cube also be a cuboid?

Yes. A cube is the special cuboid whose faces are all squares.

Can I see every face in this drawing?

No. Some surfaces face away from you; a real object lets you look around it.

Does colour decide the shape?

No. The surfaces and form matter, even when the colour changes.

Talk about it: parents & teachers

At home

  • Find a box, a ball and a safe cylindrical container.
  • Ask the child to explain a replacement using surface words.

In the classroom

  • Accept cube and special cuboid together.
  • Use physical objects to complement the flat screen drawings.
