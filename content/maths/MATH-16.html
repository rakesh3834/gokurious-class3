What’s going on here?

A pan balance compares the weights on its two sides. In this model, the heavier side goes down and the lighter side rises. When both sides have equal weight, the beam is level. Start with empty pans, then choose a side and add a labelled packet. Look at the labels to understand why the balance moves.

The packets are labelled one kilogram, half a kilogram and quarter of a kilogram. Two half-kilogram packets together have the same weight as one kilogram. Four quarter-kilogram packets also have that weight. Put one kilogram on the left and two half-kilogram packets on the right. The pans balance even though the numbers of packets are different.

The exchange button replaces one kilogram packet on the chosen pan with two half-kilogram packets. Watch whether the beam moves. It should stay at the same level or tilt because the total on that pan did not change. Removing a packet is different: it really reduces the weight on its side and can change the comparison.

Do not decide the heavier side just by counting packets or judging their drawn size. A single one-kilogram packet is heavier than one quarter-kilogram packet, even if their pictures look alike. Our balance uses the labels as exact pretend weights. Real packets and balance pans need a careful measurement. The empty pans in this model are matched, so they begin level. Try making equal combinations on both sides, then change only one packet and predict the result. You can discuss a balance at a shop with an adult or compare safe classroom objects with a teacher. Explain your prediction using the packet weights. The amount on each side, rather than the number of objects, decides whether the pans balance.

Learning outcomeI can use a balance to identify heavier, lighter and equal weights.

  2. Make an equal combination of labels on the right.
  3. Explain which side would be heavier after removing one label.

This is a paper model. Do not lift heavy objects or build a hanging balance without adult guidance.

Little questions, big discoveries

Do more packets always mean more weight?

No. Their individual weights matter too.

Why does the exchange keep the beam unchanged?

One kilogram and two half kilograms have the same weight.

Why do empty pans balance?

This model assumes matching pans with no load on either side.

Talk about it: parents & teachers

At home

  • Ask for a label-based explanation before adding a packet.
  • Discuss a shop balance while an adult handles the weights.

In the classroom

  • Distinguish packet count from total weight.
  • Use whole, half and quarter kilograms without gram conversions or force calculations.
